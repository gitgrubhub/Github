Narrative Report

**Introduction**

I am a staff psychiatrist at MGH and assistant professor at HMS. I work
in the MGH Center for Health Outcomes and Interdisciplinary Research
(CHOIR), and the MGH and MGB Salem Hospital psychiatric inpatient units.
My work leverages multidisciplinary expertise to develop complementary
therapies for the integrated health needs of underserved communities.

**Area of Excellence -- Investigation:** My research focuses on reducing
health disparities by developing complementary therapies that are
community-based, culturally relevant, and accessible to individuals from
disadvantaged communities. My interest in working with disadvantaged
communities began as an undergraduate at the University of Virginia when
I volunteered extensively with Hurricane Katrina survivors. I then
pursued an MD at Tulane University and continued to volunteer in the New
Orleans area. During my transition from medical school to psychiatry
residency, I expanded my volunteer work in disadvantaged communities to
include research by facilitating and studying a program for survivors of
the 2011 Japan earthquake, tsunami, and nuclear disaster. During my
psychiatry residency, I entered the Duke Global Health Pathway, a
structured program in conjunction with my residency program that allowed
me to pursue a Master of Science in Global Health and carry out mentored
research projects. My first research project examined the role of
spirituality among chronic kidney disease patients of rural Robeson
County, North Carolina. My second research project studied the
intersection between Nepali traditional healers and conventional
psychotherapy. I secured three internal grants to pilot investigating
this intersection within rural Central Nepal using ethnography,
semi-structured interviews, and structured observational rating scales.
I then secured funding as an NIH Fogarty Fellow to expand this pilot
across Kathmandu and rural Eastern, Central, and Far Western Nepal. Upon
returning to the US, I worked as a postdoctoral fellow in Dr. Joseph
Gone's Indigenous Community Well-Being Program at HMS to examine the
role of \"culture as treatment\" among American Indian communities. In
my current position at CHOIR, I study health disparities within the
greater Boston area by investigating how cultural adaptations of known
behavioral interventions (e.g., mindfulness-based cognitive therapy) can
better address chronic illnesses within the older Black community (e.g.,
chronic pain, depression, early cognitive decline).

Prior to joining MGH, I was awarded postdoctoral research funding from
The Josiah Charles Trent Memorial Foundation Endowment Fund, the Bass
Connections Follow-on Research Award, the Center for International and
Global Studies Graduate Award for Research and Training, the Global
Health Pathway Program at Duke University, the Fogarty Global Health
Fellowship at the NIH, and the Community Indigenous Well-Being Program
at HMS. At MGH, I completed the HMS Dupont Warren Fellowship and
received foundational funding to establish the community-based and
qualitative foundations for my work to come. I was then awarded a K23
award to develop and assess the feasibility and acceptability of
mindfulness-based cognitive therapy as culturally adapted for older
Black Americans with chronic pain and co-occurring depression ("*Quiet
Focus*") via focus groups, an open pilot, and a pilot RCT. In parallel,
I received support from the NIA Resource Center for Minority Aging
Research, NCATS/Harvard Catalyst, and the NIA R21 award mechanism to
culturally adapt mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for older Black
Americans with co-occurring chronic pain and early cognitive decline
("*Feeling of Being*"), conduct an open pilot of this developed
intervention, and then to conduct a pilot RCT comparing *Feeling of
Being* to an active comparison arm. Both the *Quiet Focus* and *Feeling
of Being* studies are currently at the pilot RCT stage.

**Teaching and Education:** I have contributed to seven courses both
regionally and internationally, individually mentored 42 research
trainees/clinicians, and led four projects to improve trainee and
physician well-being and researcher access to learning materials. During
my psychiatry residency, I developed a project to increase the sense of
community among trainees by highlighting stories of humanity within a
weekly newsletter. I also used a forensics case during my residency that
challenged provider-patient boundaries to question norms around how our
trainee programs handle ethically challenging clinical scenarios among
residents while offering several recommendations. As a Duke Global
Health and NIH Fogarty Fellow, I sought to lower the access barriers to
ethnography among researchers from low- and middle-income settings by
outlining my methodological innovations as an NIH Fogarty Fellow.
Specifically, I discussed an approach to ethnography, data collection,
and data analysis that relied entirely on open-source, free software and
affordable, widely available smart phone technology.

**Health Disparities:** My track record volunteering in communities
impacted by natural and humanitarian disasters, facilitating quality
improvement projects in clinical settings, and conducting health
disparities research demonstrates my unwavering commitment to
communities at-risk for serious illnesses. My volunteer history has
spanned New Orleans post 2005 Hurricane Katrina, Japan post 2011 triple
disaster, Nepal post 2015 earthquake, rural North Carolina, Indian
Country, and most recently the Greater Boston Area and its underserved
communities. My clinical quality improvement projects have promoted
cross-talk and community across disconnected disciplines and encouraged
delivery of healthcare to populations vulnerable to serious illnesses. I
have regularly contributed to health disparities initiatives through my
formal and informal involvement in various committees. Within the
HMS/MGH system, my MGPO Frigoletto Building Communities and Connection
Grant developed a community for MGH physicians to discuss Asian American
and Pacific Islander well-being. My research has focused on reducing
health disparities by developing, assessing, and disseminating
complementary therapies for the integrated health needs of communities
at-risk for serious illnesses (e.g., chronic pain, depression,
dementia). My current NIH and foundation grant funded research
culturally adapts mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for the chronic
pain needs of older Black Americans within the community.

**Clinical Service:** I provide psychiatric care to patients and
families within community, outpatient, and inpatient settings. I use a
holistic, culturally sensitive, and meaning-focused perspective that
blends psychopharmacology with second and third-wave cognitive
behavioral skills (e.g., acceptance and commitment therapy, dialectical
behavior therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy). I also
contribute to MGH's Psychiatry Center on Disparities, and Nutritional
Psychiatry/Lifestyle Psychiatry Group and respond to a full spectrum of
humanitarian emergencies, whether natural or man-made, through MGH's
Global Disaster Response and Humanitarian Action team. Through my
partnership with Union Capital Boston and other organizations based in
the Greater Boston Area, I regularly moderate discussions about the
chronic pain, early cognitive decline, and mental health needs of their
community. I was funded by the MGB Frigoletto Committee's Building
Communities and Connection ;Program to solidify the MGB Asian Pacific
Islander doctor community by providing an open and relaxed forum for
social and professional networking, engagement, and support.

**Summary:** My professional goal is to become an independent
clinician-scientist who develops complementary therapies for the
integrated health needs of communities at-risk for serious illnesses,
provides clinical care, and mentors other clinical investigators. I plan
to integrate research, volunteerism, practice, and training to reduce
health disparities, train the next generation of healthcare
professionals and researchers, and make meaningful contributions to
science and practice. I also hope to serve in leadership positions in
the healthcare workforce; enhance responses to humanitarian and natural
disasters; and promote community-engaged healthcare and research.
